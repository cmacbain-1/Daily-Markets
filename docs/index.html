Ten-Year Yield Back Above 5% as Stocks Slip and Bitcoin Sinks

Wall Street gave back ground on Thursday as Treasury yields jumped again, while the dollar hit a 52-week high and crypto extended its slide. Chip stocks stayed the market's wild card.

The big story

The bond market is setting the tone. The US 10-year Treasury yield, the interest rate the government pays to borrow for a decade and the benchmark for mortgages and corporate loans, rose 11 basis points (1 bp = 0.01 percentage point) on Thursday to 5.10%. The 5-year climbed 12 bp to 4.95% and the 30-year added 8 bp to 5.43%. Over a week, the 10-year is up 27 bp, though it is still 9 bp below where it stood a month ago and below its 52-week high of 5.31%.

The trigger, according to coverage earlier this week, was a hotter-than-expected October inflation report: Morningstar/MarketWatch reported on Tuesday that stocks fell as the 10-year reclaimed 5%, and another report said consumer prices rose 0.4% against forecasts of 0.3%. The same coverage pointed to growing bets that the Federal Reserve might raise rates rather than cut them. I could not verify precise market pricing, so treat that as a theme, not a number.

Why does it matter for stocks? Higher yields make safe government bonds more attractive and reduce what future company profits are worth today. That hits expensive growth shares hardest. On Thursday the S&P 500 fell 0.35% to 7,592, the Dow lost 0.36% to 52,297 and the Nasdaq Composite dropped 0.39% to 25,366. The small-cap Russell 2000, more sensitive to borrowing costs, was weakest, down 1.66% to 2,799. Even so, the S&P is up 3.25% on the week, reflecting a sharp rebound from earlier losses.

Around the world

US. Stocks drifted lower in a session dominated by bond-market worries. The VIX, Wall Street's "fear gauge" that tracks expected swings in the S&P 500, stood at 18.26 on Friday morning, down 2.35% on the day and about 29% lower than a week ago, so nerves have calmed even as the indexes wobbled. Energy was the only major sector to gain on the day.

Europe. Markets are slightly softer in Friday trading. The FTSE 100 is up 0.09% at 10,872, within sight of its 52-week high of 10,910.60. The mid-cap FTSE 250 is down 0.41% at 24,813. Germany's DAX is down 0.13% at 26,738, France's CAC 40 is down 0.12% at 8,352 and the Euro Stoxx 50 is down 0.19% at 6,387. Despite today's dip, the DAX is up 3.55% on the week and 5.34% on the month.

Asia. The picture was mostly negative. Japan's Nikkei 225 gained 0.53% to 61,142, helped by a weak yen (the dollar buys 160.03 yen, near the top of its range). Elsewhere, Hong Kong's Hang Seng fell 1.55% to 23,638 and has lost 3.45% this week. The Shanghai Composite slipped 0.59% to 3,634, the Nifty 50 in India lost 0.61% to 25,144, South Korea's KOSPI shed 0.98% to 5,291 and Australia's ASX 200 fell 0.71% to 9,082.

Sectors and stocks

Energy led, with the sector fund XLE up 1.51% and ExxonMobil gaining 1.84%. Materials (-1.28%), industrials (-0.89%), communication services (-0.96%) and financials (-0.72%) lagged. Health care and consumer staples slipped around half a percent, and utilities were flat.

Chips are where the action is. Nvidia fell 4.34% to $166.19, close to the bottom of its 52-week range and 24.90% lower over the past month. Recent coverage ties the pressure to a controversy involving the company; Morningstar/MarketWatch asked whether rivals AMD and Broadcom are the real winners. Both rose again: Broadcom jumped 5.54% to $364.36 (up almost 25% in a week) and AMD gained 5.86% to $585.30, a 44.73% weekly rise.

Elsewhere in Big Tech, Alphabet rose 1.79%, Tesla 1.69%, Amazon 0.48% and Microsoft 0.36%, while Meta fell 0.82% and Apple dipped 0.14%.

Banks were soft: Goldman Sachs fell 1.43% to $913.60, Morgan Stanley 0.76%, Bank of America 0.32% and JPMorgan 0.17%. In London, HSBC is down 0.25% and Barclays 0.61%. Among other heavyweights, Johnson & Johnson rose 1.11% to a 52-week high, Visa fell 1.23% and Eli Lilly lost 0.70%.

Rates, currencies and commodities

Beyond the long end, the 3-month Treasury bill yield is 4.13%, its highest in a year. Short rates sitting this high while long yields climb suggests markets see little chance of near-term relief from the Fed.

The US Dollar Index, which measures the dollar against a basket of major currencies, is at 103.23, a 52-week high and up 5.04% this year. The euro has fallen to $1.1051 (down 0.40% on the day) and the pound to $1.3224 (down 0.40%). Higher US yields draw money into dollars.

Oil has cooled but remains elevated: Brent is $96.49 a barrel (+0.70% today, -8.90% over a month) and WTI is $88.86. Both are still up more than 50% this year. Natural gas is $3.2870, up 9.64% on the week.

Gold, usually a refuge when investors are nervous, is struggling against rising yields: it is $3,894 an ounce, down 4.45% in a week and 10.31% this year, near the bottom of its 52-week range. Gold pays no interest, so it looks less attractive as bond yields rise. Silver is $49.92 (-12.17% over a month) and copper $6.1620.

Crypto

Crypto is weak again. Bitcoin is $69,251, down 2.93% in 24 hours, 14.58% on the month and 20.86% this year, though still above its 52-week low of 58,558.86. Ethereum fell 2.77% to $1,961. Solana is $80.75 (-1.12%) and XRP is $1.0216 (-1.76%). XRP is down 44.48% in 2026 and sits just above its 52-week low. Like growth stocks, crypto tends to struggle when yields rise and money gets more expensive.

On the radar

AMD is up 173.30% this year and Broadcom is rising while Nvidia wobbles. Nvidia reports earnings on Tuesday 17 November, according to one calendar, which could set the next direction.

India. The Nifty 50 is up 10.66% in a month, though it is still down 3.77% this year. The India ETF INDA is up 9.59% over the month. Today's dip comes amid a strong run, so it is worth watching whether foreign money keeps flowing in as US yields rise.

Term of the day

Basis point. A basis point is one-hundredth of a percentage point, or 0.01%. Traders use it because bond yields move in small steps and "percent" gets confusing. If the 10-year Treasury yield rises from 4.99% to 5.10%, it has gained 11 basis points. It also avoids ambiguity: if a rate goes from 4% to 5%, did it rise one percentage point or 25%? Saying "100 basis points" makes clear it is the former. Central banks also quote rate changes this way: a "25 bp hike" means a quarter-point increase. Today, the 5-year yield's 12 bp rise is a big daily move for a government bond.
